# Investing in AI (newsletter): Wall Street's demand for visible AI launches is pushing companies into rented-model API wrappers that give them no lasting advantage. "AI" came up on 331 of 493 S&P 500 Q2 earnings calls (67%; the ten-year average is 114). Stocks that mentioned it are up 15.7% this year vs 8.1% for those that didn't. The essay argues the durable moat is slower work restructuring the company's own data.

- Thesis: "In AI, fast means fragile." Boards now ask how fast they can announce AI, not how it helps the business. That pushes them toward licensing a frontier model, wrapping it around existing data and adding a chat window, which a competitor can copy in the same few weeks (the Red Queen effect).
- Data: FactSet counted "AI" on 331 of 493 S&P 500 Q2 earnings calls (67%), against a ten-year average of 114 calls. Companies that mentioned AI are up 15.7% on average this year, vs 8.1% for those that didn't. The author notes this may be correlation.
- The stick: sell-side "AI loser" baskets. Goldman runs a long AI-resilient / short AI-casualty software pair trade (launched in February). Jefferies has an "AI risk" basket. A JPMorgan note last week flagged Software, Business Services and Media as vulnerable to AI and stayed cautious even at near-record-cheap valuations.
- The carrot: Amazon reported $25B run rates each for its AI and chip businesses, and Microsoft said Azure had passed $100B in annual sales. Both stocks rallied (Amazon passed $3T in market cap). That kind of reward only works for companies that collect other firms' AI spend as revenue.
- Buffett's textile-loom analogy: when every mill buys the same cost-saving loom, the savings go to customers. A rented frontier model is "the new loom."
- Opportunity cost: the best engineers get pulled onto launch features, and the data-restructuring project keeps getting pushed back.
