# Investing in AI (newsletter): the AI bubble is in who pays, not the tech. It splits AI demand into five buyer markets. "Balance Sheet AI", paid from venture cash (startups sending their rounds to model, GPU and dev-tool vendors) or board-mandated enterprise pilot budgets, is a bubble. "P&L AI", which cuts an existing operating cost (support, claims triage, fraud) or does slow domain work (biopharma, chip design, grid, robotics), is durable and gets stronger in a downturn.

The five markets, from most to least bubbly:
1. VC round-trip: AI startups selling to other VC-backed startups, spending equity rather than earnings on models, cloud GPUs, vector DBs and dev tools. The essay rates it extreme bubble risk: this spend vanishes when funding tightens, and infra vendors that booked it as recurring revenue take the hit.
2. Boardroom-mandate POCs: Fortune 500 pilots (internal chatbots, summarizers) paid from exploratory budgets. High risk and already deflating; CFOs pull budgets that show no hard savings within 12-18 months.
3. Prosumer and workflow copilots (coding, transcription, writing tools): the value is real, and the risk is pricing power. Thin wrappers get squeezed by foundation models shipping the same features and by incumbent suites bundling them. Moderate risk: churn and margin compression, not collapse.
4. Embedded operational efficiency at scaled companies with distribution and proprietary data (support, catalog ingestion, claims, fraud). Low risk. Its example is Klarna, including its partial return to human agents, which it reads as calibration rather than failure.
5. Domain-specific deep work (biopharma, chip design, materials, grid maintenance, industrial robotics): adoption is gated by trials, tolerances and regulators, so it never got a hype multiple. Very low risk, an "anti-bubble."
Illustrative math: a $40M/yr support operation cut in half makes $5M/yr of inference an easy buy that survives a recession. That is unlike a startup spending its Series A on tokens.
The essay also argues that model access is now largely a commodity, so distribution matters more than investors assume.
